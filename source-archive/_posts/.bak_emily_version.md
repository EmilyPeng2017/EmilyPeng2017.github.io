硬件就是玄学。Hardware is a mystery. >_/<

Finally, after months of troubleshooting, I found the root cause.

The smart insole system runs on an STM32WB30, a dual-core ARM Cortex-M4 microcontroller. The first version of the self-designed board was working with some simple functions. After adding sequencer with low power management, i made some optimization and have the second version now.

 Getting it to talk over Bluetooth took the better part of two months (I admit I was frustrated and afraid there was no board working...), and the symptom kept changing: first nothing advertised at all, then the device appeared in the scanner but would not connect, then it connected and died after exactly five seconds, every time.

It turned out to be **three completely independent faults stacked on top of each other**, each one sufficient on its own to break Bluetooth. Fixing one only revealed the next. And before any of that, there was a hardware problem that had to be solved with a hot air gun.

# Prologue — a transplant, with a hot air gun

Before there was any firmware to debug, there had to be a board that worked.

The PCB house did not stock two of the parts on our BOM. So the boards arrived beautifully assembled, with two conspicuously empty footprints where the important things were supposed to go.

Luckily, I still have two old boards with those two items. Which turned the problem from *procurement* into *surgery*. Harvest, then transplant.

The IMU is a **BMI088** in an LGA-16 package. If you have never met one: it is a little black rectangle about the size of a grain of rice, and **all sixteen of its pads are underneath it**. There is nothing on the sides for a soldering iron to touch. You cannot see the joints while you make them. You cannot see them after you make them either. 

So the procedure goes:

1. **Flux everything.** Then flux it again. Flux is not a step, it is a lifestyle.
2. **Hot air to lift the donor part** off the old board, without cooking the four neighbours sitting 2 mm away — which mostly means moving the nozzle in small circles and resisting the urge to turn the temperature up.
3. **Clean both sets of pads** — the part's and the new board's. Old solder has to come off; wick and patience.
4. **Fresh solder paste** on the destination footprint, a thin even film.
5. **Place the part** roughly straight — roughly is genuinely enough.
6. **Hot air again**, and wait.

All the steps above are what I learned from Bilibili, thank you!!!

Step six is the good part. When the paste goes liquid, surface tension pulls the component into alignment all by itself. The chip visibly *snaps* square onto the footprint. It takes about half a second and it is, I maintain, the single most satisfying moment available in hardware work.

Then comes the uncomfortable bit: you have no idea whether it worked. Sixteen joints, all hidden under the part, and no X-ray in the lab. Continuity testing is not an option when there is nothing to probe. The only test that exists is an electrical one, power it up and ask the chip who it is.

Which is why the first two lines of our boot log are the ones I care about most:

```
BMI08 initialization success!
Accel chip ID - 0x1e
Gyro chip ID  - 0xf
```

`0x1E` and `0x0F` are the values straight out of the BMI088 datasheet. Reading them back over SPI means the power pads made contact, the ground pads made contact, and all four SPI lines made contact. **A chip ID register is the cheapest and most honest test of a hand-soldered joint you will ever get**.

The IMU lived. The board was real.

I hate to say that, but it took me a whole Saturday to fix the first board and make the IMU work. It sucks.

# Part 1 — Method

## The observable is not binary

"Bluetooth doesn't work" is useless. On BLE there are at least four distinct states, and each one points at a different part of the system:

| Observation | What it rules in |
| --- | --- |
| Nothing in the scanner | The radio never started, or the advertising command failed |
| Visible, **not connectable** | Advertising is running but declared non-connectable, or filtered by a whitelist |
| Visible, connectable, connection **refused** | The link layer rejects — parameters, security, resource limits |
| Visible, connects, then **drops silently** | The link is fine; something above it stopped answering |

One thing I want to mention, one capacitor which works for the antenna was blowed away by the hot air gun and I couldn't find it. 

## Split the system where it is actually split

An STM32WB is not one processor. It is two, and they do not fail together, what I learned:

- **CPU2 (Cortex-M0+)** runs the wireless coprocessor binary — the entire BLE link layer *and* host. Once you have issued `aci_gap_set_discoverable()`, CPU2 advertises autonomously. **It keeps advertising even if CPU1 has crashed.**
- **CPU1 (Cortex-M4)** runs your application. It talks to CPU2 over IPCC mailboxes.

This has a direct diagnostic consequence that is easy to miss: *a device that is visible in the scanner does not prove your application is alive.* It only proves CPU2 is alive. And because CPU2 needs CPU1 to drain the IPCC event buffers, a dead CPU1 will eventually starve CPU2 too, advertising first continues, then stops. I built a whole hypothesis around that mechanism before the logs ruled it out.

So: **decide which processor you are accusing, and find an observable that separates them.**

## first, make the silent parts speak, but know what the instrumentation costs

The default ST project ships with `CFG_DEBUG_APP_TRACE = 0`, which compiles out every `APP_DBG_MSG()`. That is why the console was silent about the single most important fact in the whole investigation. The first useful action was not a fix; it was adding `printf()` along the chain:

```
CPU2 ready → APP_BLE_Init() → SHCI_C2_BLE_Init() → aci_gap_set_discoverable()
```

Two traps here, and I fell into both.

**Trap one: newlib buffers semihosting stdout.** The log gets truncated at an arbitrary point and looks exactly like a hang. One line fixes it:

```c
initialise_monitor_handles();
setvbuf(stdout, NULL, _IONBF, 0);
```

**Trap two — the expensive one: a semihosting `printf()` is a `BKPT 0xAB`.** It halts the core until the debugger services it, for tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Put one inside an HCI event callback and i have stalled CPU1 at precisely the moment the link is being negotiated. **My instrumentation becomes the bug.**, it was half of fault #3 below.

The fix:

```c
/* Callbacks only push into a ring. main() drains and prints. */
void diag_trace(uint8_t ev, uint16_t a, uint16_t b)
{
  uint8_t w = diag_tr_wr;
  diag_tr[w].t = HAL_GetTick();
  diag_tr[w].a = a;  diag_tr[w].b = b;  diag_tr[w].ev = ev;
  diag_tr_wr = (uint8_t)((w + 1u) % DIAG_TR_N);
}
```

## A constant interval is a timer, not a decision

This is the single observation that cracked the final fault. Seven connection attempts, and the interval between "connected" and "disconnected" was:

```
5.029   5.017   5.000   5.037   5.000   5.032
```

Nothing in software fails at exactly 5.000 s six times in a row. Constant intervals mean a timeout fired, which tells me **the peer gave up waiting, nobody actively refused anything.** 500 × 10 ms is Android's default supervision timeout, and `status=8` is HCI `0x08 Connection Timeout`. That reframed the question from *"why is it rejecting me?"* to *"what silenced the ATT layer in the first few milliseconds?"*, and the second question has a much smaller answer space.

## Read the log for what is absent

The Android log showed `Starting service discovery: true` and then nothing. Crucially it showed **neither**:

- `Service found with UUID: ...` — discovery succeeded, nor
- `Main service could not be found.` — discovery succeeded but returned an empty database

Both branches are logged in our app. Neither appeared, so `onServicesDiscovered` never fired at all. An empty GATT database would have produced the second line. That single absence eliminated a whole family of hypotheses about the service definitions.

<img src="/img/blogs/44_stm32wb_ble_debug/logcat-annotated.svg" alt="The signature in logcat" style="width:100%;">

## Change one variable — and verify it is the variable you think

I disabled `CFG_LPM_SUPPORTED` to test a low-power hypothesis. No change. It is tempting to conclude "low power is not involved," but that is wrong: **`CFG_LPM_SUPPORTED` only controls whether CPU1 enters Stop mode.** CPU2 sleeps between radio events regardless and always uses the configured low-speed clock. The test eliminated one specific mechanism, not the whole category. Being precise about what a negative result actually excludes is as important as running it.

Similarly: for a long time "Debug works, Release doesn't" framed the search. It dissolved the moment I asked a blunt question — *had a connection ever actually succeeded in Debug?* It had not. Nobody had tested connecting in Debug; "Debug works" meant "the boot log looks right." **Check that your control condition is really a control.**

# Part 2 — The three faults

## Fault 1 — CPU2 was running FUS, not the BLE stack

**Symptom:** nothing advertises. The semihosting log stops at `Operational mode change request to: 12` and never prints `Operational mode changed to: 12`.

**Why that line is diagnostic.** The second message lives in `apply_operational_mode()`, which only runs when the sequencer task `CFG_TASK_OPERATIONAL_MODE` is queued. The only place that queues it is inside `APP_BLE_Init()` — and `APP_BLE_Init()` is only called from the `WIRELESS_FW_RUNNING` branch of the CPU2 ready event. So the missing line does not mean the operational-mode code is broken. It means **CPU2 never reported a running BLE stack.**

Two ways to end up there, and I hit both:

1. **A factory-fresh STM32WB ships with FUS only — there is no BLE stack on it.** You have to flash one. Two brand-new boards behaving identically is expected, not a firmware bug.
2. **CubeProgrammer's "Read FUS infos" switches CPU2 to FUS and never switches it back.** FUS then stays active across power cycles. So the very act of checking which stack is installed *breaks* the thing you were about to test. I did exactly this, which is why the board that used to advertise intermittently went permanently quiet in the middle of the investigation.

<img src="/img/blogs/44_stm32wb_ble_debug/fus-infos.png" alt="CubeProgrammer Firmware Upgrade Services panel" style="width:100%;">

Reading that panel at all is what leaves FUS running. `FUS State = FUS_IDLE` with a populated `STACK Version` means the stack is *installed*, not that it is *executing*.

**Fix:** Firmware Upgrade Services → **Start Wireless Stack**, then power-cycle. Repeat after every "Read FUS infos".

For a 512 KB WB3x part, the stack this project needs is `stm32wb3x_BLE_Stack_full_extended_fw.bin` at **0x08053000** (tick *First install* on a virgin part). The version string does not distinguish the variants, so verify through **Option Bytes → SFSA**, which is the secure-flash start address in 4 KB pages:

| SFSA | Install address | Variant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `0x53` | 0x08053000 | **full_extended** — has extended advertising |
| `0x5C` | 0x0805C000 | full — no extended advertising |

I needed `full_extended` because `CFG_BLE_OPTIONS` sets `SHCI_C2_BLE_INIT_OPTIONS_EXT_ADV`, and the broadcaster mode uses `CFG_BLE_MAX_ADV_DATA_LEN = 1650` — far beyond the 31 bytes a legacy advertisement can carry.

## Fault 2 — four application-layer defects that never start advertising

With a healthy stack, still nothing. Four separate problems, and **fixing any one alone changes nothing** — which is exactly why they survived so long.

**(a) The boot mode has BLE switched off, and cannot be left.**

```c
case OP_MODE_TESTING:                       // the mode entered at boot
    configure_ble(&op_mode_conf->ble_conf,
                  OP_BLE_DISABLE,           // ← BLE off
                  GAP_PERIPHERAL_ROLE, 1);
```

and `update_operational_mode()` has no `case OP_MODE_TESTING`, so it falls through to `default` and the device can never transition out. BLE is off forever.

**(b) Even with (a) fixed, the advertising branch is unreachable.**

```c
ble_activity_t prev_enable = OP_BLE_ENABLE;   // ← initial value
...
if ((ble_conf->enable == OP_BLE_ENABLE) &&
    ((prev_enable == OP_BLE_DISABLE) || (ble_conf->GAP_role != prev_GAP_role))) {
        Adv_Request(APP_BLE_FAST_ADV);        // never reached on the first call
}
```

At boot `prev_enable` is already `OP_BLE_ENABLE` and the role has not changed, so neither disjunct holds. Initialising it to `OP_BLE_DISABLE` makes the first call take the branch.

**(c) With (a) and (b) fixed, the board hangs.** That branch called `APP_BLE_Init()`, which issues a **second** `SHCI_C2_BLE_Init()` — the first already ran when CPU2 signalled ready. CPU2 accepts it once per reset; the second returns an error and the stock error path is

```c
void Error_Handler(void) { __disable_irq(); while (1) {} }
```

with no watchdog enabled anywhere in the project. One transient failure and the board is dead until someone pulls the power. **If your `Error_Handler()` is an infinite loop and you have no watchdog, every bug becomes a silent brick.**

**(d) `disable_ble()` destroyed the GATT database.** It called `hci_reset()`, which wipes the GATT database and GAP configuration — but nothing rebuilds them, because `Ble_Hci_Gap_Gatt_Init()`, `SVCCTL_Init()` and `Custom_APP_Init()` only run inside `APP_BLE_Init()`. After one mode switch the device would still advertise while exposing no services. It also gated the IPCC clock off, breaking every later HCI command.

> A footnote that cost us a day: these four fixes had been applied a month earlier and were **silently reverted by a merge**. `app_ble.c` was byte-for-byte identical to the original again. The firmware directory was not under version control. It is now.

## Fault 3 — an ACI command fired from inside an HCI event callback

Now the device advertised, connected — and dropped after exactly 5.0 s, on every board, in every build. This is the fault the constant interval pointed at.

```c
void handle_connection_complete_event(...)   /* runs inside the HCI event callback */
{
    printf("Connection established:\n");
    printf("\t- Central: @:%02x:%02x:%02x:%02x:%02x:%02x\n", ...);
    printf("\t- Connection Handle:   0x%x\n", Connection_Handle);
    ...
    // Handle any incoming MTU requests        ← this comment is wrong
    aci_gatt_exchange_config(Connection_Handle);
}
```

**The comment is the bug.** `aci_gatt_exchange_config()` does not *handle* incoming MTU requests — those are answered autonomously by the GATT server on CPU2, and need no application code at all. What the function does is make this peripheral act as a **GATT client and send its own ATT Exchange MTU Request**.

So at the instant the link comes up, the phone is issuing `requestMtu(156)` and the device is issuing an Exchange MTU Request of its own. ATT permits one Exchange MTU procedure per connection and one outstanding transaction per direction. The two collide, the ATT layer never recovers, service discovery goes unanswered, and five seconds later the supervision timer fires.

<img src="/img/blogs/44_stm32wb_ble_debug/connection-sequence.svg" alt="How the link dies: the MTU collision" style="width:100%;">

There is a clue to this hiding in plain sight in the log: `MTU size changed to 156` appears **5 ms** after connection. A real over-the-air exchange needs at least one connection interval — 30 to 50 ms at Android's initial parameters. 5 ms is too fast, because what the phone was reporting was *our* request arriving, not its own being answered.

On top of that, the call was made from inside `hci_user_evt_proc()`'s context — the very sequencer task that `BLE_StatusNot()` pauses with `UTIL_SEQ_PauseTask()` for the duration of an outstanding command. And the three semihosting `printf()` calls beside it stalled CPU1 for tens of milliseconds right in the connection window, which is why *no* board could connect in the Debug build. `aci_gatt_write_permit_req_event()` was worse still — one `printf()` **per data byte**, also inside a callback.

**Fix:** delete the `aci_gatt_exchange_config()` call, and move every `printf()` out of every HCI callback into the ring buffer.

# Part 3 — What to keep

The diagnostic harness is worth more than the fixes, because the fixes are specific and the harness is not.

- **`diag_trace()` / `diag_trace_flush()`** — event codes recorded in callbacks, printed from `main()`. Near-zero cost in release.
- **A heartbeat** — `[HB] <ms>` every 5 s from the main loop. If it stops while the device is still advertising, CPU1 has died and CPU2 is carrying on alone. That one line distinguishes two hypotheses that otherwise look identical from outside.
- **`setvbuf(stdout, NULL, _IONBF, 0)`** — so a truncated log never masquerades as a hang.
- **Branch tracing at the CPU2 handshake** — print which branch the ready event takes (`WIRELESS_FW_RUNNING` vs `FUS_FW_RUNNING`) and the firmware versions. Fault 1 would have been a five-minute problem with this in place.

# Takeaways

1. **Describe the symptom precisely before theorising.** "Visible but drops after 5 s" and "can't connect" are different failures with disjoint causes.
2. **On a dual-core SoC, name the processor you are accusing.** Advertising proves CPU2 is alive. It proves nothing about CPU1.
3. **Never `printf()` from an HCI event callback**, and never send an unsolicited ACI command from one. Record to RAM, print from the main loop.
4. **A constant interval is a timer.** Measure it; it usually names the mechanism outright.
5. **Read logs for absences.** The line that *should* be there and isn't often eliminates more than the lines that are.
6. **Know what a negative result actually excludes.** `CFG_LPM_SUPPORTED = 0` disables CPU1 sleep, not CPU2's.
7. **Verify your control condition.** "It works in Debug" was never true; nobody had tested it.
8. **An `Error_Handler()` that spins forever, with no watchdog, converts every transient fault into a silent brick.** At minimum, reset.
9. **Put the firmware under version control.** A month of fixes vanished in one merge and nobody noticed until file sizes were compared byte for byte.
10. **Give every hand-soldered part an electrical self-test.** On a package whose pads you cannot see, a chip ID read is the only inspection you have. Print it at boot, before you need it.

Still on the list: `save_calibration_data()` writes flash from inside an HCI callback, which on an STM32WB means contending with CPU2 for the flash semaphore and stalling the bus. Same class of mistake as fault #3, different trigger path. That one is waiting for the next calibration run.
